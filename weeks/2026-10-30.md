## How social influence can undermine the wisdom of crowd effect

Lorenz, Rauhut, Schweitzer and Helbing, PNAS 2011. This is not an agentic AI paper. It was picked as a foundational study of collective intelligence.

### Summary

A crowd's average or median guess is often close to the truth, because individual errors cancel out (Galton's weight-guessing example is the classic case). Real groups, though, talk to each other. Does that help or hurt? The paper treats the wisdom of crowds as a statistical effect and looks at how social influence changes the statistics of the group's estimates. It fills two gaps in earlier work on consensus: those studies used questions without a correct answer, and they did not pay people for being right.

The experiment had 144 participants in 12 groups of 12. Each person answered six factual questions over five rounds and was paid for accuracy. They also rated their confidence before and after each round. There were three conditions: no information about others, the average of the others' estimates, or all of the others' estimates.

Without information, estimates stayed diverse. With the average shown, estimates converged and a "wisdom of crowd indicator" (high when the group behaves like independent estimators) dropped clearly. With full information it dropped slightly. The paper names three effects:

- **Social influence effect.** The diversity of estimates shrinks, but the group's collective error does not improve.
- **Range reduction effect.** The truth moves toward the edge of the range of estimates, so the crowd becomes less useful to an outside observer.
- **Confidence effect.** People become more confident after their estimates converge, although they are no more accurate.

The presenter found the confidence effect the most interesting: agreement builds confidence whether or not the group got any closer to the truth. The authors suggest this hardening may be even stronger for opinions with no predefined correct answer, which they did not test.

### Questions

- **Does the payment bias the result?** Participants were paid for being close to the truth, so someone who sees a better-looking guess might copy it to earn more. LLM agents might do the same with a reward function. The design avoided competition: pay depended only on a participant's own estimate, never on others'. Payment came at the end of the five rounds, and participants never saw the true answer during the session. They were told beforehand that accuracy is paid, so some bias may remain. A person far from the group's estimate may expect no reward and move toward the group.
- **Was confidence shared with the others?** No. Confidence was a self-reported Likert score and was not shown to other participants.
- **Does this hold for AI agents?** One participant expects the same dynamic when LLM agents see each other's answers. The paper does not test it.

## Consensus-Driven Multi-Agent Cognitive Reasoning for Enhancing the Emotional Intelligence of Large Language Models

AAAI 2026.

### Summary

LLMs recognize basic emotions, but they are weak at cognitively demanding emotional tasks, such as inferring someone's hidden intentions or mental state in a social situation (the gap that the EmoBench benchmark points to). The paper proposes MACRo. Instead of asking one LLM to classify the emotion, five specialized agents each produce one step of a "Cognitive Chain of Thought": Situation, Clue, Thought, Action and Emotion. A coordinator agent checks the outputs, and a consensus game makes each step consistent with the step before it.

They tested 11 LLM configurations on EmoBench in English and Chinese, and compared MACRo with a single LLM doing the same reasoning. The results were mixed. One model improved from 34 to 47, DeepSeek improved little, and some models got worse. The authors conclude that the benefit depends on the model, the language and the benchmark.

The presenter liked that splitting emotional reasoning across specialized agents makes the process more structured and interpretable. The concern is that the consensus game rewards consistency between the agents, not correctness. A chain of reasoning can be perfectly consistent and still wrong.

### Discussion points

**About the paper.**

- DeepSeek may have gained so little because its single-LLM baseline was already high on this benchmark, which leaves no room to improve.
- The consensus game checks that the five reasoning steps agree with each other. Nothing in the method checks that the final emotional reading is right.

**Is "it depends on the model" a result?** The conclusion of the paper led to a long argument that went beyond it.

- *The concern.* In many agentic AI papers, the abstract promises a concrete effect and the conclusion says the effect is model dependent. That is almost always true, in the way "every human is an individual" is true. If a result can be reproduced by asking a coding assistant to run the experiments for a day, publishing it at a top venue might be stacking papers on papers without a convincing scientific story. Technical documentation or a blog might be the better place for it.
- *What it still gives us.*
  - A record of what was tried, so others do not redo it from scratch, and a better picture of how LLMs differ and fail.
  - A map of which model suits which task. With many models on the market, this can save the cost of trying each one, including hardware cost.
  - "Model dependent" should often be read as "architecture and training dependent." That tells you what you must keep the same to replicate a result, or to deploy an agent system at scale.
  - Negative results matter. If an established model of a social process fails on new data, that is a real finding.
  - Science moves in small steps. Many minor variations may add up to a breakthrough years later. One suggestion was to write a paper arguing that model-dependent studies are not the right way forward.
- *The reply.* A first study of this kind is useful. When hundreds of groups repeat it with one or two more models each, it is not clear what each addition contributes. Not everything is worth publishing, and the bar is a convincing story. By analogy, a new phone is a little better than the last one, and a paper that augments earlier work is also an incremental update.
- *Where the group landed.* There was no agreement. One view was that both kinds of work are needed, some people on fundamentals and others on incremental development. The new difficulty is that AI can now write papers, so papers are produced in bulk and can hide the results worth noticing like a fog. Individual projects can differ, but at some point the results have to be synthesized.

## Open questions

- What makes a model-dependent result worth publishing as a paper rather than as documentation?
- Who does the synthesis when papers are produced in bulk, and how do we recognize it?
- Does the confidence effect, where agreement raises confidence without raising accuracy, appear in LLM agents?
- How should we check the correctness of an agent pipeline whose consensus mechanism only enforces consistency?
